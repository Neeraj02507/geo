1. Introduction

Unsaturated soils have pores partially filled with water and air. Their mechanical and hydraulic behavior depends on suction, water content and the pore structure. The Soil–Water Retention Curve (SWRC) — θ = f(ψ) — is the fundamental relation that quantifies how volumetric water content (θ) varies with suction (ψ).

Uses: design & analysis in geotechnical engineering (slope stability, bearing capacity), environmental engineering (landfills, contaminant transport), hydrology (infiltration, recharge), and agriculture (irrigation scheduling).

2. Soil Suction — Concept & Components

2.1 Physical meaning

Suction is an energy potential difference causing water to be retained in pores. It increases as the soil dries and acts opposite to gravity.

2.2 Types

  • Matric suction (ψm) — due to capillary and adsorptive forces; most important in soils. ψm = ua − uw.
  • Osmotic suction (ψo) — due to solutes; important in saline soils or leachates.
  • Total suction — ψ = ψm + ψo.

2.3 Units & typical ranges

Suction is commonly reported in kPa (or m of water). Typical ranges: 0–2000 kPa for many soil applications; tens to hundreds of kPa for field conditions; >1000 kPa corresponds to strongly bound water.

3. Soil–Water Retention Curve (SWRC)

3.1 Definition & shape

SWRC describes volumetric water content θ as a function of suction ψ (or matric head h). Key shape features:

  • Saturation plateau near ψ ≈ 0 (θ ≈ θs).
  • Transition/air entry zone where θ falls steeply.
  • Residual plateau where θ ≈ θr at high suctions.

3.2 Physical interpretation

Large pores empty first (drain under low suction) — controls steepness. Small pores and adsorbed films remain at high suction — control residual water.

3.3 Important points on the curve

  • Air-entry value (AEV) — suction at which desaturation begins.
  • Field capacity — moisture after drainage by gravity (commonly at ψ ≈ 10–33 kPa depending on definition).
  • Wilting point — plant-available water limit (ψ ≈ 1500 kPa commonly used).

5. Empirical & analytical models for SWRC

Common models used to fit SWRC data:

  • van Genuchten (1980) — flexible 4-parameter model, widely used.
  • Brooks-Corey (1964) — simpler, power-law type useful for coarse soils.
  • Fredlund–Xing — capable of fitting extremes and residual better; used in geotechnical contexts.

Brooks–Corey

θ(ψ) = θs(ψ/ψb)-λ for ψ > ψb (ψb = air-entry)

Fredlund–Xing (brief)

More parameters to fit both wet & dry ends and to model hysteresis. Commonly used in geotechnical modeling of suction-dependent shear strength.

6. Van Genuchten model — details & fitting

θ(h) = θr + (θs − θr) / [1 + (α h)n ]m , m = 1 − 1/n

Parameter roles (recap)

  • θs — saturated water content (porosity).
  • θr — residual water content.
  • α — related to AEV; 1/α ≈ characteristic suction (kPa).
  • n — controls pore-size distribution and slope.
  • m — shape parameter derived from n for simpler fitting.

Fitting procedure (practical)

  1. Measure θ vs ψ data in lab (pressure plate, tensiometers, dewpoint, etc.).
  2. Use non-linear least squares (Python `scipy.optimize.curve_fit`, Matlab `lsqcurvefit`) to estimate θr, θs, α, n (m = 1 − 1/n).
  3. Check residuals & R²; if residual end poorly captured, consider Fredlund–Xing or adding θr constraint.

Goodness of fit

Report R², RMSE and parameter confidence intervals. Plot θ vs log(ψ) to visually inspect the fit across decades of suction.

7. Hysteresis in SWRC

SWRC depends on wetting/drying history. The drying curve (desorption) and wetting curve (adsorption) form a hysteresis loop. Main causes: contact angle, ink-bottle pores, film flow and trapped air.

Practical implications

  • Hydraulic conductivity differs on wetting vs drying.
  • Modeling infiltration after antecedent moisture requires hysteresis-aware models (e.g., Mualem-based with scanning curves).

Modeling approaches

Use main drying & main wetting curves plus scanning rules (e.g., empirical scaling, domain decomposition) to simulate hysteresis.

8. Measurement methods & laboratory equipment

Common lab methods

  • Pressure plate apparatus — used for moderate to high suctions (tens to hundreds kPa).
  • Pressure membrane (chamber) — for higher suctions (up to thousands of kPa) using axis-translation.
  • Tensiometers — measure low suctions (0–80 kPa) with high accuracy.
  • Dew point potentiameter — measures very high suctions via dew point (up to several MPa).
  • Filter paper method — simple, low-cost hygrometric technique for certain ranges.

Typical testing steps (pressure plate)

  1. Sample saturation & equilibration.
  2. Apply suction step (apply pressure), wait for equilibrium water content.
  3. Record θ at each applied suction to build θ(ψ) points.

Practical tips

  • Use sealed conditions to avoid evaporation during long equilibrations.
  • For disturbed samples, expect variability — test replicates.
  • Temperature control improves reproducibility (suction is temperature-dependent).

9. Hydraulic conductivity & retention coupling

Unsaturated hydraulic conductivity k(θ) or k(ψ)

k decreases as soil dries. Models combine SWRC with relative permeability functions to estimate k(θ) — e.g., Mualem–van Genuchten coupling.

k(θ) = ks · Krel(θ)

Mualem–van Genuchten formulation (brief)

Relative permeability Krel(Se) is derived from integral expressions involving SWRC; the van Genuchten parameters feed directly into these relations, allowing computation of k(ψ).

Practical use

  • Compute infiltration / Richards equation simulations using parameterized SWRC and k(θ).
  • Calibrate ks (saturated) from permeameter tests, then compute unsaturated k.

10. Effective stress in unsaturated soils

Classical concept

For saturated soils: σ' = σ − u. For unsaturated soils, several definitions exist due to separate air and water pressures.

Bishop's effective stress

σ' = (σ − ua) + χ(ψ) · (ua − uw)

χ(ψ) is a weighting function (0 ≤ χ ≤ 1) often related to degree of saturation; χ approximates degree of saturation or other empirical functions.

Implications

  • Shear strength increases with matric suction (up to a limit).
  • Strength models (e.g., extended Mohr–Coulomb) include suction term: τ = c' + (σ − ua)·tanφ' + (ua − uw)·tanφb.

11. Applications — contamination & vadose zone flow

SWRC influences contaminant migration by controlling water content, flow paths, diffusion rates, and retention:

  • Lower θ reduces advective transport but can increase diffusive retardation.
  • Retardation factor R depends on soil-water content via partition coefficients and θ.
  • Modeling requires coupling Richards' equation (unsaturated flow) with advection-dispersion-reactive transport.

12. Slope stability & rainfall-triggered failures

Rainfall infiltration increases pore-water pressures and reduces matric suction, reducing effective stress and shear strength; SWRC controls how quickly suction reduces for a given rainfall event.

Modeling approach

  1. Use SWRC + k(θ) to simulate infiltration (Richards equation).
  2. Compute suction distribution and convert to effective stress.
  3. Perform stability analysis (finite element / limit equilibrium) using time-varying suctions.

13. Landfill liners, covers & evapotranspirative systems

Design of compacted clay liners and evapotranspirative covers depends on retention properties. SWRC allows estimating infiltration reduction, long-term moisture content and gas transport characteristics.

Key design role

  • Choose soil with appropriate θ(ψ) to minimize percolation.
  • Estimate long-term performance under wetting-drying cycles.

14. Agricultural applications

SWRC helps determine plant-available water (PAW = θ at field capacity − θ at wilting point), irrigation scheduling, and root-zone moisture dynamics.

Definitions

  • Field capacity (~ψ = 10–33 kPa depending on convention)
  • Permanent wilting point (~ψ = 1500 kPa)

15. Numerical modeling: Richards' equation & coupling

Richards' equation governs unsaturated flow:

∂θ/∂t = ∇·[k(θ) ∇(ψ + z)]

Requires k(θ) and θ(ψ) (SWRC) for closure. Solve using finite element or finite difference methods (e.g., COMSOL, HYDRUS, MODFLOW-Richards, custom Python implementations).

16. Parameter estimation & data fitting

Use non-linear regression to estimate α, n, θs, θr. Typical workflow:

  1. Collect θ(ψ) lab data (replicates across suction decades).
  2. Use log-scale suction for fitting (ψ varies over orders of magnitude).
  3. Constrain θs (porosity known) or θr (if measured) to ensure stable fits.
  4. Report parameter standard errors, RMSE & R².

18. Instrumentation & field sensors

Field & lab instruments include tensiometers, dielectric moisture probes (e.g., TDR/FD), heat dissipation sensors, and soil water retention chambers. Each instrument has accuracy ranges and calibration needs.

  • Tensiometer: accurate 0–80 kPa, requires water-filled tube — not for dry soils.
  • TDR/Capacitance probes: measure dielectric permittivity → infer θ, need calibration by soil type.
  • Dewpoint: measures very high suctions through vapor equilibrium.

19. Practical tips & pitfalls

  • Always test replicates — soils are heterogeneous.
  • Control temperature — suction depends on T.
  • Beware of air entrapment when wetting samples rapidly.
  • Use scanning curves for repeated wetting/drying cycles.
